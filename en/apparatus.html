Apparatus

Triaxial Rock Testing System

The greatest difference between rock and other materials is that rock is commonly found under a high confining pressure, and its mechanical behaviour under high confinement is quite unlike its behaviour at low confinement. Under high confining pressure the strength rises markedly, and the rock also changes from a brittle to a ductile material. The triaxial testing system is therefore the single most important instrument in rock mechanics.

The triaxial testing system of this laboratory is an RTX-1000 machine manufactured by GCTS, USA. The instrument was donated by our alumnus Sheng-Biao Chen and purchased through Prof. Tai-Tien Wang. Its maximum load is 1000 kN (about 100 tonnes) and its maximum confining pressure 70 MPa (equivalent to a depth of about 2000 m), which is sufficient for triaxial testing of rock under the geological conditions normally encountered in Taiwan. The tests it can perform include (1) uniaxial compression tests, (2) triaxial tests, and (3) indirect tensile tests.

The instrument is not at present offered as an external service. If you wish to use it, please contact the head of the laboratory.

Rock Core Cutter

The rock core cutter is an important piece of equipment for preparing specimens for triaxial testing, since it trims cores to a size suitable for the test procedure.

The cutter of this laboratory was designed by a domestic manufacturer. It uses a non-draining design with the cooling water held internally, and can cut core samples up to 60 mm in diameter.

Point Load Tester

The point load tester is a small instrument for rock mechanics testing, originally developed at Imperial College London. It consists of a two-column fixed crosshead frame together with a manual hydraulic jack. Pressure applied by the jack drives the conical platens forward and delivers a point load; from the load at which the specimen fails, the uniaxial compressive strength of the rock material can be estimated.

The tester of this laboratory is an EL-77 point load tester made by ELE, United Kingdom. Its maximum applied load is 55 kN and the largest specimen diameter it accepts is 101.6 mm.

Ultrasonic Pulse Velocity Tester

The ultrasonic tester measures the ultrasonic wave velocity of a material; through the relationship between velocity and density, the dynamic elastic modulus of the body can be obtained indirectly.

The tester of this laboratory is an HC-6390 ultrasonic tester made by Humboldt, USA. It conforms to ASTM C597 and has a measuring range of 0–3000 ± 0.1 μs.

Instruments for Field Investigation

Field investigation is an essential part of rock mass engineering and the first step towards understanding the characteristics of a site. Field work often takes place in remote locations where large instruments cannot be carried, so a number of small instruments are needed to obtain preliminary information about the rock mass. The small instruments held by this laboratory are listed below.

Schmidt Hammer

The Schmidt hammer is an instrument for estimating the strength of concrete, and it can also be used to obtain the uniaxial compressive strength of rock material.
The hammer of this laboratory is a C380 made by MATEST, USA. Its impact energy is 0.225 m-kg, and it is suitable for materials with a uniaxial compressive strength of 10–70 MPa.

Geological Compass

The geological compass, also called a clinometer, measures the attitude of strata and is one of the instruments indispensable to field investigation.
The compass of this laboratory is a Type 55 clinometer made by IWAMOTO MINERAL, Japan, measuring 80 × 66 × 20 mm.

Geological Hammer

The geological hammer is an essential field tool. By striking and scraping the rock it gives a first estimate of the strength of the material, and it is also used to obtain fresh rock samples.

Profile Comb

The profile comb is a tool for measuring the roughness of a joint surface. When the comb is pressed against the surface, the metal rods rearrange themselves to follow its undulations, so that the profile of the joint can be traced onto paper.

Feeler Gauge

The feeler gauge measures the aperture of a joint. It consists of a set of metal leaves of differing thickness which can be inserted into a joint during field investigation to measure its opening.

Surveying Instruments

Surveying is one of the foundations of geotechnical and resources engineering, and good survey results are a powerful aid to the analysis and design that follow. The surveying instruments held by this laboratory are listed below.

Level

The level is an instrument for measuring the difference in elevation between observation points, and is used chiefly for levelling on engineering sites.
The level of this laboratory is an AT-F6 made by Topcon, Japan, with a measurement error of 2.5 mm per kilometre.

Theodolite

The theodolite is a mount with two coordinate axes that can be supported and rotated, allowing horizontal and vertical angles to be measured in surveying. It is an important tool in engineering survey work.
The theodolite of this laboratory is an NT-2D made by Nikon, Japan.

Total Station

The total station is a modern optical-electronic surveying instrument that combines a theodolite with an electronic distance meter. From a single set-up it can complete the measurement of horizontal angles, vertical angles, distances and differences in elevation, which is how it came by its name.
The total station of this laboratory is a TS06-plus made by Leica, Germany. Its working range is 5–150 m, with an orientation accuracy of 5 cm at 100 m.

GPS Receiver

The Global Positioning System (GPS) was originally developed by the United States for military positioning and navigation; the programme was later extended so that it could be applied to civilian positioning and surveying. As the GPS satellites travel through space they broadcast signals towards the ground continuously. Users on the ground receive these signals with a satellite receiver and, by exploiting the different characteristics of the signals, obtain the distance between satellite and receiver and the baseline vectors between the receivers on the ground. Geometric principles then give the position of each receiver, which completes the work of navigation, positioning and surveying.
