Numerical Analysis

The Discrete Element Method

The discrete element method (DEM) is a comparatively young tool of numerical analysis. It was first proposed by Cundall in 1971 to analyse the progressive failure of rock blocks. The method computes the motion of the elements through a force–displacement relationship and examines the interaction between particles and its consequences, so that the questions the researcher is interested in can be explored. Its range of application is extremely broad: depending on whether bonds between particles are introduced, the method is able to represent both continuous and discrete bodies. The problems it can treat fall roughly into the following classes:

(1) The motion of granular materials
        For example the transport and deposition of debris flows, landslides and rockfalls in geomaterials subjected to seismic loading.
(2) Bearing capacity and fracture behaviour
        For example the simulation of rock specimens under compression, the analysis of soil bearing capacity, the study of slope stability, and the stability analysis of tunnel rock masses.
(3) The mechanical behaviour of highly frictional blocks
        For example the simulation of direct shear tests, the analysis of the sliding potential and run-out of dip slopes, and the reproduction of the fault dislocation process.
(4) Complex problems combining two or more of these characteristics
        For example the simulation of jointed rock masses, which are both highly frictional and prone to fracture, or the reproduction of a dip slope that disintegrates after sliding and develops into a debris flow.

The Particulate Interface Model

An “interface” here means any contact surface between solids, and is not restricted to geological weak planes such as joints or cleavage. The contact between a rock bolt and the surrounding rock, between a pile and the soil, or between one mineral grain and another within a rock material may all be called an interface. In rock engineering the mechanical properties of the interfaces govern the stability of the rock mass, which makes the discrete element method — able as it is to represent interface mechanics — the better choice for analysis. The most advanced interface model currently available in the discrete element method, the smooth joint model, nevertheless does not account for the geometrical arrangement of the particles during shearing or for its relation to the input parameters, and therefore cannot provide accurate simulations of interface behaviour.

To resolve this problem our group has considered the contact and separation characteristics of a group of particles under shear, introduced a number of corrections into the calculation, and incorporated the two failure criteria most widely used in practice — the Mohr–Coulomb and the Barton–Bandis criteria. The result is an accurate interface model for the discrete element method, which we have named the Particulate Interface Model (PIM). PIM reproduces interface friction accurately within a discrete element analysis, and the input parameters it requires can be taken directly from laboratory test data without back-calculation or adjustment. This makes the discrete element method a more credible tool for predicting hazards in rock engineering: it can be applied to the stability analysis of dip slopes, used to build a discrete element framework for rock support systems, and employed to study the deformation modes of slate and schist. The range of extensions is wide, and it offers engineers and researchers a more complete set of analytical perspectives.

The Biconcave Bond Model

The mechanical behaviour of rock material is one of the central topics of rock mechanics. Unlike soil, rock possesses a considerable cohesion, and the nature of the cementation between the grains is an important factor governing its mechanical behaviour. While examining thin sections of rock we observed that the cementation between grains approximates a biconcave shape. Starting from this observation we developed a bond model with a biconcave cement geometry, which we named the Biconcave Bond Model.

The biconcave bond model gives a physically reasonable description of the response of a biconcave cement under load, and from the material and geometrical parameters supplied it yields the stress distribution, the stiffness and other aspects of the bond response. When specimens of known geometry and fabric are simulated by back-analysis, the micro-scale material parameters used by the biconcave bond model lie closer to the values measured in laboratory tests. This indicates that the model performs better when macroscopic mechanical behaviour is to be predicted from micro-scale material parameters together with the fabric of the specimen.

Simulation of Jointed Rock Masses

We tend to regard the rock surrounding a geotechnical work as a solid foundation, and to assume that its mechanical properties are homogeneous and isotropic when planning the design and analysis that follow. That view is appropriate for an intact block of rock, or for a rock mass so heavily fractured that it behaves as a continuum. Once a rock mass contains one or more sets of joints, however, its mechanical behaviour is no longer homogeneous and isotropic but anisotropic. Strength and deformability then differ with direction and the behaviour becomes highly complex. Jointed rock masses are consequently very difficult to deal with in engineering design, and they represent one of the principal challenges of rock mass engineering.
